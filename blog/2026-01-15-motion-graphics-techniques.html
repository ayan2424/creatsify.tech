10 Essential Motion Graphics Techniques Every Designer Should Know

ER

Emma Rodriguez

Motion Graphics Lead
January 15, 2026 12 min read 3.8K views

Whether you're just starting out in motion graphics or looking to refine your skills, mastering these ten fundamental techniques will dramatically improve the quality of your work. These principles are what separate amateur animations from professional-grade motion design.

The Foundation of Great Motion Design

Before diving into specific techniques, it's important to understand that great motion graphics are built on the same principles that have guided animators for decades. The 12 principles of animation, first introduced by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas, remain as relevant today as they were in 1981.

1 Easing (Ease In & Ease Out)

The most common mistake beginners make is using linear motion. Nothing in the real world moves at a constant speed. Easing adds acceleration and deceleration to your animations, making them feel natural and polished.

Pro Tip: In After Effects, use the Graph Editor to fine-tune your ease curves. Try starting with an "easy ease" and adjusting from there.

2 Anticipation

Before any major action, there should be a smaller, opposite movement. A button about to bounce will first compress slightly. A character about to jump will crouch down first. This prepares the viewer for what's coming and makes the action feel more impactful.

3 Squash & Stretch

This technique adds weight and flexibility to your animations. When an object hits the ground, it squashes. When it bounces up, it stretches. The key is to maintain consistent volume—as an object gets wider, it should get shorter, and vice versa.

4 Follow-Through & Overlapping Action

Not everything stops at the same time. When a character stops running, their hair and clothes will continue moving for a moment. In UI animation, secondary elements should have slight delays, creating a more organic feel.

5 Timing & Spacing

The number of frames between two poses (spacing) dramatically affects how an animation feels. Close spacing = slow movement. Wide spacing = fast movement. Mastering timing is arguably the most important skill in animation.

"Animation is not about moving things around. It's about bringing things to life through movement and timing."

6 Secondary Action

These are smaller movements that support the main action. If a logo is bouncing into frame, perhaps there's a subtle shadow moving beneath it, or particles trailing behind. Secondary actions add depth and interest without distracting from the primary animation.

7 Arc Movement

Most natural movements follow curved paths, not straight lines. When animating position, consider whether an arc would make the movement feel more natural. This is especially important for anything organic or character-related.

8 Staging

Clear staging ensures your audience knows where to look. Use contrast, scale, and movement to direct attention. One strong action is better than multiple competing elements.

9 Overshoot & Settle

When an object arrives at its destination, having it overshoot slightly and settle back creates a satisfying, bouncy feel. This technique is essential for UI animations and logo reveals.

10 Rhythmic Motion

Great motion graphics have a rhythm, almost like music. Movements should work together, creating visual beats that guide the viewer's experience. Consider syncing key moments to audio or establishing your own visual tempo.

Putting It All Together

These techniques work best when combined thoughtfully. A simple text animation becomes captivating when you add proper easing, a slight anticipation before the reveal, some overshoot as it lands, and follow-through on any secondary elements.

Start by focusing on one or two techniques at a time. Once they become second nature, gradually incorporate more. With practice, you'll develop an instinct for what each animation needs to feel right.

Recommended Practice

  • Recreate animations you admire, paying attention to the techniques used
  • Animate a bouncing ball—it's a classic exercise that incorporates many principles
  • Study real-world movement and try to recreate it digitally
  • Get feedback from other motion designers and iterate
